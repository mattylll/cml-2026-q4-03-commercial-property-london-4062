Commercial Property London in 2026: 4,062 Sales, 12.11 Billion Pounds and What Changed

Ninety buildings. That is the gap between the 530 London sales at 5 million pounds or more in 2024 and the 440 in 2025, and it explains most of what happened to commercial property London-wide last year. Total value dropped by 2.21 billion pounds, from 14.32 billion to 12.11 billion. The number of deals dropped by 305, from 4,367 to 4,062. Value fell about twice as fast as volume, and yet the price of the typical building hardly shifted: the median went from 730,000 pounds to 700,000. Put those three facts side by side and the story is not a market that got cheaper. It is a market that sold fewer of its biggest lots while the ordinary shop, office and workshop carried on trading. Below we take the count apart by price band, show where the money sits, and explain what the count includes and what it leaves out.

How the count is built

Start with the method, because every figure after it depends on it.

HM Land Registry’s Price Paid Data sorts sales by property type. One type, “other”, holds everything that is not a single house or flat: shops, offices, industrial units, pubs, mixed-use buildings, land and whole blocks. Greater London had 5,738 such records in 2025.

We then removed what plainly was not a commercial or non-standard building:

  • 521 records whose address was a flat
  • 260 garages, parking spaces and moorings
  • 749 priced under 50,000 pounds

Portfolio sales, where one price is recorded against every title in the portfolio, were counted as one deal. That left 4,062.

So when this article says “sales”, it means commercial and non-standard property sales on that definition. It is not a count of every commercial sale in London, and the section on limits further down explains why.

Three price bands, three different years

The report gives two thresholds, 1 million and 5 million pounds. Subtract them from the totals and you get three bands. The band figures below are our own arithmetic on the report’s numbers.

Read down the last column. The further up the price scale you go, the harder the fall. Sales under 1 million pounds slipped by about 5 percent. Sales at 5 million or more fell by 17 percent.

The mix shifted as a result. Sales at 5 million pounds or more were about 12 percent of all deals in 2024 and about 11 percent in 2025. That sounds small. It is not small in money, because those are the lots that carry the total.

London did not get cheaper in 2025. It sold 90 fewer very large buildings, and that alone explains why value fell twice as fast as deals.

A median of 700,000 pounds and a mean close to 3 million

Two averages describe this market, and they are a long way apart.

The median, the price of the middle deal, was 700,000 pounds. Half of everything sold in London in 2025 was priced below that.

The mean is total value divided by the number of deals. For 2025 that is 12.11 billion pounds over 4,062 sales, which comes to about 2.98 million pounds. For 2024 it is 14.32 billion over 4,367, about 3.28 million. The mean is our calculation and does not appear in the report.

A mean more than four times the median tells you a small number of very large sales sit on top of a very large number of modest ones. The mean fell by roughly 300,000 pounds between the two years. The median fell by 30,000.

For a borrower, the median is the number that matters. It describes the part of the market that term commercial mortgages fund: shops with flats above, small offices, light industrial units and single mixed-use buildings.

Four boroughs, 43 percent of the money

Westminster, Camden, Kensington and Chelsea and the City of London produced 43 percent of London’s total value from 16 percent of its deals.

Add the rows: 654 sales and about 5.21 billion pounds. Westminster alone accounts for 3.36 billion, more than a quarter of the London total, from 293 deals.

Take those four out and the other 29 boroughs shared 3,408 sales and about 6.90 billion pounds. That is the London most buyers are dealing in.

All four slowed in 2025. Westminster’s sales were down 10.1 percent, Camden’s 10.3 percent, Kensington and Chelsea’s 18.4 percent and the City of London’s 21.6 percent. When the boroughs that hold the value slow down, the London total follows, whatever is happening on a high street in the outer boroughs.

What the count leaves out

The sales figure has limits, and the report states them plainly.

Company sales are missing. Many large buildings are sold by selling the company that owns them. Those deals never reach Price Paid Data. This is why the City of London shows only 58 sales while having the highest median in London. A low count there reflects how the Square Mile trades.

Lettings are missing. A new lease at a market rent is not a sale.

Some residential stock is in. The figure includes some residential blocks and development sites sold as single lots.

Medians reflect mix. A borough median moves with what happened to sell as much as with prices. The report does not publish a median or a yearly change for any borough with fewer than 30 deals in either year, and we do not estimate one.

Late registrations. A small number of 2025 sales may still be added.

Read the count as a consistent yardstick of non-residential and non-standard freehold and long-leasehold trading. It is comparable between boroughs and between years. It is not a census.

One more caution. A falling deal count is not a signal to avoid a borough. Fewer sales can mean less competition for stock. For a lender it means fewer recent comparables, so the valuation carries more weight.

2026 so far

When the data was cut on 17 November 2026, HM Land Registry had registered 1,245 commercial and non-standard sales completed in London during 2026. Their median price was 675,000 pounds.

That count is partial. Sales are registered months after they complete, so it will keep rising well into 2027. It cannot be set against a full year, and we do not do so.

The median is the more useful early sign. At 675,000 pounds against 700,000 for the whole of 2025, it suggests typical lot sizes have held.

The other 2026 evidence comes from a different register. Companies House recorded 20,817 company charges on London property between January and September 2026, up 18.9 percent on the same months of 2025. Those charges cover all property that companies borrow against, most of it residential investment, so they show lender activity and not commercial sales. But they do say lenders have been busier in London this year, not quieter.

What a buyer at the 2026 median is looking at

This is illustrative arithmetic on a hypothetical property, not a quote.

Take a let building bought at 675,000 pounds, the partial 2026 median. A commercial investment mortgage is indicatively available at 65 to 75 percent loan to value across our lender panel. At 65 percent the loan is 438,750 pounds and the deposit 236,250. At 75 percent the loan is 506,250 pounds and the deposit 168,750.

The indicative investment rate band is 6.5 to 8.5 percent a year. On 506,250 pounds that is between about 32,900 and 43,000 pounds of interest a year. The lender will then want the rent to cover the interest by 140 to 160 percent at a stressed rate, so the rent, and not the valuation, often sets the final loan.

In 2025, 1,133 of the 4,062 sales, 28 percent, were matched to a company charge at Companies House. That is a floor, because individuals, partnerships, pension schemes and most overseas buyers do not file charges.

Outlook

The Bank of England raised Bank Rate from 3.75 percent to 4 percent at the meeting ending 4 November 2026, and the next decision is due on 17 December 2026. We will not know the 2026 sales total until the registrations are in. Until then, the fair summary is a steady median, a lighter top end and busier lenders.

FAQ

How many commercial properties were sold in London in 2025? On our filtered count of HM Land Registry records, 4,062 commercial and non-standard properties, worth 12.11 billion pounds. That excludes buildings sold through a company sale, so the true number of commercial deals is higher.

What does a typical commercial property in London cost? The median sale in 2025 was 700,000 pounds. Medians by borough run much higher in the centre, at 2,315,000 pounds in Westminster and 2,420,000 in the City of London.

Did London commercial property fall in value in 2025? Total sales value fell 15.4 percent, but mostly because fewer large buildings sold. The median slipped 4.1 percent, and a median reflects the mix of what sold as much as any change in price.

Which borough has the most commercial property sales? Westminster, with 293 in 2025. Croydon was second with 231, more than any other borough outside Westminster, at a median of 530,000 pounds.
